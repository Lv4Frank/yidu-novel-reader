The Lantern Keeper

Chapter 1  The Harbor at Dusk

The old harbor smelled of salt and rusted iron. Mira stood at the edge of the pier, watching the last fishing boat slide through the fog like a patient ghost. She had come here every evening for three years, and still the sea refused to give her an answer. The lighthouse on the far cliff blinked twice, then darkness.

"You are late," said a voice behind her. Mira did not turn around. She knew the voice too well: low, careful, with the ruined warmth of a man who had spent too many winters alone. "The lamps are lit," she said. "That is all that matters."

They walked together along the wet stones. The town was quiet in the way that towns become quiet after a long illness. Somewhere a dog barked once, and a window went dark. Mira counted the steps to the lighthouse door, as she always did, and found the same number: one hundred and forty-seven.

Chapter 2  A Letter Without a Stamp

The letter had been waiting under the door for two days. It was addressed in a hand she had not seen for a decade, and the ink had smeared where the rain had reached it. Mira carried it to the kitchen table and set it down without opening it. The kettle sang. The clock in the hall ticked, and ticked, and ticked.

When she finally opened it, a single sheet of paper fell out, folded three times. It contained no greeting, no signature, only a map drawn in careful lines and a sentence: "The garden you remember is still there. Come before the first snow."

Chapter 3  The Long Road

The road climbed through fields of grey grass and broken fences. At every mile marker Mira stopped to rest her shoulders and to listen for the sound of water. On the third day she met a traveler who carried a cage of white birds, and he told her that the mountains ahead were full of old stories that no one had bothered to write down. She asked him whether he knew the name of the garden, and he only smiled and walked on.

Chapter 4  First Snow

The garden was smaller than she remembered, but the stone bench was in the same place, and the pear tree still leaned toward the wall as if listening to a secret. Snow began to fall at noon, softly at first, then in earnest. Mira sat on the bench and waited. She had waited three years already; an afternoon more seemed almost nothing. When the gate opened, she did not look up. She only said, quietly, "You came."

The End.
